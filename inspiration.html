Inspiration

Old ideas about trust, tested against computer science

Fitrah takes its name, and several research prompts, from Islamic thought about trust, stewardship and accountability. This page shows how each idea maps onto models computer science already has.

A rule we hold ourselves to: naming a familiar access-control technique after a beautiful concept doesn’t make it new. Every inspiration is checked against prior art first. These are human moral and legal teachings; none of them is a specification for software, and nothing here is a religious ruling.

Scale isn’t novelty either. Google’s Zanzibar already checks relationship-based permissions at enormous scale, and agent delegation is an active research area (IETF WIMSE and 2026 preprints). The bar for a new contribution is high.

A candidate research question

Obligations that outlive the agent

A waqf separates the people who administer an endowment from the conditions that govern it. That suggests a question for agents: when an agent restarts, is replaced or delegates, should the obligations attached to a resource survive, and can that be enforced?

The question

  • Under crashes, retries, delegation, credential rotation and revocation, can resource obligations be preserved and enforced?
  • Example: a healthcare agent processes records for a declared purpose and delegates to a child that may restart. The purpose restriction must survive.

How it would be tested fairly

  • Compare a strong usage-control broker with obligations against the same broker plus Fitrah’s hereditary rules, on identical fault traces.
  • If the strong broker achieves the same properties, report equivalence instead of renaming the same rules.
  • Only checkable resource operations: no claims about ethics or intent.

Interpretive limits. Waqf practice developed over centuries, with recognised and disputed adjustments, so “never amend” is not an accurate reading. Usage-control obligations also predate Fitrah. Whether this question leads anywhere new is open.
